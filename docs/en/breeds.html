  • Scottish Fold

    Known for forward-folded ears and an owl-like expression. Many are sweet and adaptable — but the gene that folds the ears also affects cartilage throughout the body.

    • Coat: Short
    • Activity: Moderate
    • Size: Medium
    Full profile

    Coat
    Short or long, dense coat
    Grooming
    Weekly brushing; check ears regularly
    Activity
    Moderate
    Size
    Medium (roughly 3–6 kg)
    Health considerations
    Important welfare note: the fold mutation causes osteochondrodysplasia — abnormal cartilage and bone development that can lead to painful, progressive joint disease in folded cats. Several veterinary and welfare bodies discourage breeding for folded ears. If you love this look, consider adopting a cat in need and ask a vet about joint care.

Cat Care Guide

Everything a cat needs to thrive

A practical, evidence-informed overview. It is not a substitute for advice from your veterinarian, who knows your cat.

Nutrition

Cats are obligate carnivores: they need nutrients found in animal tissue, such as taurine. Choose a complete and balanced food labelled for your cat’s life stage. Measure portions, avoid frequent treats, and never offer onions, garlic, chocolate, grapes, alcohol or lilies (lilies are highly toxic to cats).

Hydration

Many cats drink little by nature. Offer fresh water in several wide bowls away from the food and litter, consider a fountain, and include wet food if your vet agrees. Hydration matters even more in hot climates.

Grooming

Brush according to coat type — daily for long coats, weekly for most short coats. Check claws, ears, eyes and teeth during calm grooming sessions. Hairless cats need gentle bathing to manage skin oils.

Litter box hygiene

A common guideline is one box per cat plus one, in quiet, easy-to-reach places. Scoop daily and wash boxes regularly with mild, unscented products. Sudden changes in litter habits can signal a medical problem.

Indoor exercise & enrichment

Indoor cats need to hunt, climb, scratch and explore. Offer wand-toy play sessions, vertical spaces, scratching posts, food puzzles and safe window views. Rotate toys to keep them interesting.

Veterinary checkups

Healthy adult cats generally benefit from at least one wellness exam a year; kittens and senior cats often need more frequent visits. Cats hide pain well, so routine exams catch problems early.

Vaccination

Core vaccines usually protect against feline panleukopenia, herpesvirus and calicivirus, and rabies where required by law — as in Dubai. Your vet will tailor any additional vaccines to your cat’s lifestyle.

Neutering

Spaying and neutering prevent unplanned litters, reduce roaming and some behaviour problems, and support efforts to manage stray populations. Ask your vet about the right timing.

Common warning signs

Contact a vet promptly for changes in appetite, weight, thirst or litter habits; vomiting or diarrhoea that persists; hiding; or limping. Seek emergency care for breathing difficulty, straining in the litter box without passing urine, collapse or suspected poisoning.

Senior cat care

From around age 11, cats are often considered senior. Provide soft, warm beds, low-entry litter boxes and steps to favourite places, and ask about more frequent checkups including blood and blood-pressure tests.

Safe indoor environments

Fit secure screens on windows and balconies, tidy away cords, string and small objects, store medicines and cleaning products safely, and check that houseplants are non-toxic before bringing them home.

Cat care FAQ

How much should my cat eat?

It depends on age, weight, activity and the food’s energy content. Start with the label’s guidance, weigh your cat regularly and ask your vet to help you set a target.

Is milk good for cats?

Most adult cats are lactose intolerant, so cow’s milk can cause stomach upset. Fresh water is the best drink.

Do indoor cats need vaccines?

Yes. Viruses can enter on shoes and clothing, and cats may escape or need boarding. In Dubai, rabies vaccination is also part of the legal registration process.

How do I know if my cat is in pain?

Look for hiding, reduced grooming, a hunched posture, squinting, reluctance to jump or changes in appetite. If in doubt, contact your vet.
